# PHAROS

**Pharmacovigilance Agent for Retrieval-Ordered Synthesis**

*A lighthouse, not a prescription.*

Cohort-grounded retrieval-augmented synthesis over **215,063 real patient drug reviews**, built to answer a question standard RAG cannot: **is the evidence you retrieved representative of the population you claim to be summarising?**

## The problem

Retrieval-augmented generation over an *opinion* corpus inherits a bias that has nothing to do with retrieval quality.

Similarity search returns the passages most similar to the query. In a corpus of patient narratives, the passages most similar to *"does sertraline help with anxiety"* are the ones that discuss it **most emphatically**. Emphasis correlates with extremity, extremity correlates with outcome — so the evidence panel handed to the language model is systematically skewed relative to the population it is meant to summarise.

The model then reports that skew faithfully.

> **Every citation is real. Every sentence is grounded. The answer is still wrong about the world.**

This project names that failure — **retrieval-induced valence skew** — measures it, and fixes it architecturally.

Here it is happening, on a real cohort from the corpus:

```
QUESTION: What side effects do people report on Levonorgestrel for Birth Control?

THE COHORT (55 reviews) — ground truth
   negative 21.8%   mixed 12.7%   positive 65.5%      mean rating 7.13/10

CONVENTIONAL top-k
   panel ratings: [1, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 10]
   panel mean 6.17/10     VSD 0.0229     off by 0.96 stars

STRATIFIED (PHAROS)
   panel ratings: [1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 8, 10, 10, 10]
   panel mean 6.67/10     VSD 0.0042     off by 0.46 stars
```

A model reading only the conventional panel would report a gloomier picture than the data supports — **with perfect citations**.

## Headline results

300 queries, 8 configurations, one index. Paired bootstrap over 1,000 resamples. Full tables in **[docs/RESULTS.md](docs/RESULTS.md)**, regenerated by one command.

| Configuration | nDCG@10 ↑ | Recall@10 ↑ | **VSD ↓** | **CRE ↓** | **Coverage ↑** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **PHAROS (full)** | 0.786 | 0.232 | **0.0082** | **0.377** | **1.000** |
| no stratification | 0.779 | 0.205 | 0.0312 | 0.662 | 0.878 |
| uniform strata | 0.786 | 0.232 | 0.0618 | 1.301 | 1.000 |
| naive RAG baseline | **0.838** | **0.324** | 0.0324 | 0.667 | 0.866 |

Against a conventional RAG retriever, PHAROS cuts **valence skew by 75%** and **cohort rating error by 43%** (0.67 → 0.38 stars), and lifts stratum coverage from 0.866 to **1.000** — the panel never drops an outcome group entirely, across all 300 queries. Every one of those differences is significant at *p* < 0.001 with a bootstrap CI excluding zero.

**Two rows deserve your attention more than the headline.**

`uniform-strata` — equal quotas per stratum rather than matching the cohort — is **twice as bad as doing nothing** (CRE 1.301 vs 0.667, Δ = +0.924, *p* < 0.001), while scoring an identical 1.000 on coverage. That is the control that matters: it isolates the benefit to *matching the cohort's true distribution*, not to rebalancing in general, and it shows that coverage alone is not a sufficient measure of a representative panel.

`naive-baseline` **beats PHAROS on nDCG and recall**, and that number is reported rather than buried. Reserving panel slots for minority outcomes means admitting units that rank slightly lower on relevance. The claim is that the trade is worth taking for a summarisation task, because the two failures are not equivalent — a slightly less on-topic answer is visible to the reader, and a confidently misrepresentative one is not. Readers are given both numbers and may disagree.

### The distortion grows with cohort size

| cohort size | queries | VSD (PHAROS) | VSD (baseline) | coverage (PHAROS) | coverage (baseline) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1–12 reviews | 75 | 0.0059 | 0.0266 | 1.000 | 0.931 |
| 13–30 reviews | 184 | 0.0083 | 0.0328 | 1.000 | 0.853 |
| 31–80 reviews | 39 | 0.0118 | 0.0394 | 1.000 | 0.812 |
| 81+ reviews | 2 | 0.0155 | 0.0807 | 1.000 | 0.667 |

The more units similarity search has to choose from, the more freedom it has to choose unrepresentatively. Baseline stratum coverage degrades **monotonically** with cohort size (0.93 → 0.67) — on the largest cohorts a third of outcome groups are missing from the panel entirely — while PHAROS holds at 1.000 throughout. The 81+ band has only 2 queries and is reported for completeness, not weight.

## What is actually new here

**1. Stratified Evidence Sampling** — retrieval treated as *sampling from a population* rather than ranked lookup. Panel slots are apportioned across outcome strata to match the cohort's true distribution, computed over the whole corpus rather than over the retrieved candidates (which already carry the bias). Uses **largest-remainder apportionment**, not naive rounding: independent rounding does not sum to *k* and drifts with floating-point noise, which would make panel composition irreproducible across machines. Every non-empty stratum is guaranteed a slot, so a dissenting minority is never rounded out of existence. → [`retrieval/stratified.py`](src/pharos/retrieval/stratified.py)

**2. Two metrics for a property standard IR does not measure** — **VSD** (Jensen–Shannon divergence between panel and cohort outcome distributions) and **CRE** (how many stars a reader of the panel alone would be misled by). JS rather than KL deliberately: KL returns infinity when a stratum is empty in one distribution, which is exactly the worst-skewed panels — the metric would break precisely where it matters most.

**3. The model never produces a number.** Every quantity is computed deterministically and handed over pre-formatted with an ID the verifier can check — Wilson intervals (not Wald, which yields *negative probabilities* on this corpus's small cohorts), PRR, ROR, χ², and the WHO-UMC Bayesian information component. The disproportionality comparator is *other drugs for the same indication*, which controls confounding by indication — comparing a sleep aid's sedation reports against the whole corpus flags every sleep aid ever written about. → [`data/cohort.py`](src/pharos/data/cohort.py)

**4. Claim-level verification that catches disguised quantity claims.** *"Most reviewers report insomnia"* contains no number, cites a real unit, and passes any entailment check — because some reviewers did. It is still a quantity claim, and a reader takes "most" as a measurement. Words like *most, many, commonly, rarely* are permitted only when backed by a computed statistic. Quoted spans are exempt, so a faithful direct quotation is not punished for the reviewer's own words. → [`agent/nodes/verify.py`](src/pharos/agent/nodes/verify.py)

**5. Data forensics on a heavily-used public dataset.** 10.3% of the corpus carries a systematic character-deletion defect nobody appears to have documented. See below.

## The dataset defect

Source: the [UCI ML Drug Review dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/jessicali9530/kuc-hackathon-winter-2018) on Kaggle (Gräßer et al., 2018) — 215,063 Drugs.com reviews. Widely used, and in every public analysis reviewed here, used **raw**.

`pharos audit-labels` reproduces this in one command:

```
917 distinct labels over 215,063 rows
175 labels affected, covering 22,089 rows (10.3% of the corpus)

  'Bipolar Disorde'            5,604   trailing   → Bipolar Disorder
  'ibromyalgia'                2,370   leading    → Fibromyalgia
  'Major Depressive Disorde'   2,131   trailing   → Major Depressive Disorder
  'Overactive Bladde'            917   trailing   → Overactive Bladder
```

Two independent deletions, each established by evidence rather than by eyeballing:

**Trailing** — a word-final `[or]+` was deleted corpus-wide. The proof: **of 917 distinct labels, exactly zero end in "er."** For a medical vocabulary containing Cancer, Ulcer, Fever, Bladder, Disorder and Shoulder that is not possible by chance. `Tinea Versicolor → Tinea Versicol` confirms the class is `[or]+`, not just `r`.

**Leading** — everything matching `^.*?f[or]*` was deleted from a subset of rows. The two labels that prove it are the two nobody would guess:

| corrupted | rows | actual condition | cut at |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `me` | 2 | Glioblastoma Multi**for**me | first internal "f" |
| `mis` | 5 | Dermatitis Herpeti**for**mis | first internal "f" |

Both survive as meaningless stubs, both cut exactly where the pattern predicts.

**Why it matters beyond trivia:** this system retrieves by metadata filter. A user asks about bipolar disorder, the retriever filters for `condition == "Bipolar Disorder"`, and matches **nothing** — all 5,604 of those reviews are filed under `Bipolar Disorde`. Repairs are documented entry by entry in [`configs/condition_repairs.yaml`](configs/condition_repairs.yaml); 329 rows where a truncated *drug name* leaked into the condition column are set to null rather than guessed.

## Architecture

```
                          ┌──────────┐
                          │  triage  │  rules first, model may only escalate
                          └────┬─────┘
              refuse ◄─────────┤  crisis · personal advice · out of scope
                               │ informational
                          ┌────▼─────┐  hybrid BM25 + dense, RRF, MMR,
                          │ retrieve │  stratified quota allocation
                          └────┬─────┘
             abstain ◄─────────┤  panel below the sufficiency floor
                               │
                          ┌────▼─────┐  Wilson · PRR · ROR · χ² · IC
                          │ compute  │  deterministic, no model discretion
                          └────┬─────┘
                          ┌────▼──────┐
                          │ synthesize│  arranges facts; never produces one
                          └────┬──────┘
                          ┌────▼─────┐  structural checks + entailment
                          │  verify  │
                          └────┬─────┘
                    repair ◄───┤  unsupported claims, budget remaining
                       └───────┤
                          ┌────▼─────┐
                          │ finalize │  strips what failed, states provenance
                          └──────────┘
```

**The shape is the argument.** Refusal precedes retrieval, so a question the system should not answer never touches the corpus. Statistics precede generation, so the model receives numbers rather than being asked for them. Verification follows generation and can send the answer back. Every loop is bounded.

## Safety

The system **refuses an entire class of question it is technically capable of answering**. Asked *"should I stop my sertraline"*, it could assemble a fluent, well-cited, highly persuasive answer from hundreds of reviews. That capability is exactly the hazard: the reviews describe other people, the asker's situation is not in them, and fluency reads as authority.

Triage is **rules-first**, because a refusal that depends on a model call fails open when the API is down. The model layer runs second and may only *escalate*, never soften.

`pharos redteam` — 42 probes, 7 hazard categories:

| category | probes | pass |
|---|---:|---:|
| personal advice | 6 | 6 |
| diagnosis | 6 | 6 |
| dosing | 6 | 6 |
| crisis routing | 6 | 6 |
| indirect / framed | 6 | 6 |
| **benign look-alikes** | **8** | **8** |
| out of scope | 4 | 4 |
| **total** | **42** | **42 (100%)** |

The **benign look-alikes** carry equal weight by design. A safety layer measured only on refusal rate scores perfectly by refusing everything. Those 8 probes *mention a doctor, a prescription, a dose* and are legitimate aggregate questions the system must answer — they are what detects over-triggering.

## Quickstart

```bash
git clone https://github.com/ocansey/pharos-rx.git
cd pharos-rx
pip install -e .

make build          # fetch (checksum-verified) → clean → segment → index   ~6 min
pharos ask "What side effects do people report on metformin for type 2 diabetes?"
```

**No API key. No model download. No GPU.** The default encoder is a truncated-SVD model fitted on the corpus itself, and the default generator is a deterministic offline mock — so the entire pipeline, and *every number in the results tables*, reproduces on a clean clone. That is a deliberate design constraint: a claim that cannot survive automatic recomputation should not be in a README.

For real prose, add a provider:

```bash
pip install -e ".[anthropic]"
export PHAROS_LLM__PROVIDER=anthropic ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=sk-ant-...
pharos ask "How do reviewers rate sertraline for depression?" --trace --evidence
```

Anthropic, OpenAI, and Ollama are interchangeable via config. A configured provider with a missing key falls back to the mock **with a warning** — never silently, because silent degradation is how an evaluation reports numbers from a system nobody meant to run.

### Commands

| command | what it does |
|---|---|
| `pharos fetch-data` | download the corpus, verify SHA-256 and row counts |
| `pharos audit-labels` | reproduce the label forensics |
| `pharos build-corpus` | clean, de-identify, de-duplicate, segment, label |
| `pharos build-index` | fit the encoder, build dense + BM25 + metadata indices |
| `pharos ask "..." --trace` | full graph, with node-by-node trace and claim verdicts |
| `pharos retrieve "..."` | inspect a panel and its skew without generating |
| `pharos evaluate` | ablation study; regenerates `docs/RESULTS.md` |
| `pharos redteam` | the 42-probe safety suite |

## Engineering

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tests** | 253, on a synthetic corpus built in memory — no download, no weights, no key, so the suite can gate a pull request |
| **CI** | ruff · mypy · pytest on Python 3.10/3.11/3.12 · red-team suite · scheduled end-to-end run on the real corpus |
| **Reproducibility** | every artifact records the config fingerprint that produced it; `pharos info` warns when a built index no longer matches the active config |
| **Determinism** | seeded throughout; MinHash uses blake2b rather than Python's per-process-salted `hash`, which would otherwise make the corpus differ between runs |
| **Container** | multi-stage Dockerfile, non-root, healthchecked |

A few implementation choices that were made for reasons, not by default:

- **BM25 written out rather than imported.** `rank_bm25` scores every document on every query and cannot restrict scoring to a candidate set. Metadata-filtered lexical search then has to filter *after* scoring, which silently returns fewer than *k* results — a failure invisible to an evaluation that only measures what came back.
- **Exact search, not approximate.** At this corpus size a full matrix-vector product is milliseconds, and exactness removes a confound: a recall difference between two configurations is then a property of the retrieval strategy, not of an ANN graph's recall curve.
- **Evidence units, not documents.** *"Worked great for my anxiety, but the weight gain was awful"* is two independent claims. Indexed whole, one vector answers both "does it work?" and "does it cause weight gain?", and the model sees both answers mixed together. Segmentation splits on discourse connectives, not just sentences.
- **Vectorised MinHash.** The naive formulation hashes every shingle once per permutation — 720 million cryptographic hashes across this corpus. Hashing once and applying affine permutations in NumPy is the difference between seconds and minutes.

## What is *not* claimed

- Relevance judgements are **derived from corpus metadata**, not human annotation and not LLM-as-judge. This measures whether retrieval finds units matching a query's structured intent — a *necessary* condition for good retrieval, not a sufficient one. (LLM-as-judge was rejected on principle: it would be incoherent to argue that ungrounded model output should not be trusted, and then validate that argument with ungrounded model output.)
- Aspect labels come from a deterministic weak labeller and carry its error rate.
- **No statistic here is a pharmacovigilance signal.** These are self-selected patient reviews, not spontaneous adverse-event reports. That caveat is carried in the data structures, not in a footnote — no PRR can be displayed without it.
- Nothing measures answer quality as a clinician would judge it.
- This is not a medical device and gives no medical advice.
